Hansel Valley: The Questions No One Has Studied

Posted Oct. 31, 2026 · Updated Oct. 31, 2026
The date to know

You can protest until Tuesday, Dec. 1, 2026.

Not Nov. 4. Nov. 4 to 11 is just when the notice runs in the newspaper.

To protest, go to the Utah Division of Water Rights "Protest of Application" page and type in a55167. It costs $15, and you don't get it back. You can ask for a hearing on the form.

What it is

A data center and a giant power plant want a ranch's spring water.

For more than 100 years (since 1904), water from Salt Wells Spring in Hansel Valley has watered ranch land from April to October.

A new filing asks to pump that water all year to:

  • a power plant rated at 7,500 megawatts, and
  • a data center campus.

How much water:

1,712 acre-feet a year
How we got here

They tried once already.

  • March 2026: the ranch filed to move part of this water.
  • By May 5: the state's record lists 3,678 protests.
  • May 6: a new law (H.B. 60) narrowed what the state can weigh to mostly water pollution and water scarcity.
  • May 7: that first filing was withdrawn.
  • Oct. 15: the new filing came in, for all of the water.

What your protest can talk about: water supply, water quality and harm to other water users. That's what the State Engineer weighs now.

The ground

The spring sits on active faults.

Spring water is underground water coming up through cracks in the rock. The developer's own water expert says so.

In 1934, a magnitude 6.6 earthquake hit Hansel Valley. It's the only quake in Utah's history known to break the ground's surface. When it hit:

  • New springs showed up.
  • Old dry wells started flowing again.
  • Other wells stopped for hours.
  • Some land sank as much as 39 cm (about 15 inches).

Utah's fault database lists 212 faults, fault sections and folds (2003). The three Hansel faults are in the top category.

What the developer's own papers say

Their filing answers some questions and skips others.

  • Two different power plants. One page says cleaner gas turbines. The engineer says piston engines, a type estimated to put out about 6½ times more of a smog-making pollutant.
  • Wastewater. Treated cooling-tower water is planned to be released into a stream or other surface water. Which one isn't decided.
  • Faults. One sentence, from a 1980 map. The 1934 quake isn't mentioned.
  • "Geologic testing" means soil testing for where to build. Faults aren't on their list of problems.
  • The underground water wasn't measured. They checked streams and a pond on two summer visits in 2026, a year with Utah's lowest snowpack on record.
  • Too hot to finish. In July 2026, their crew skipped some checks "due to high forecasted temperatures." That's the valley today, before any plant.
  • Water for workers only. The water plan counts 4,850 employees. Hotels, restaurants, RV parks and homes are allowed there, and we found no water line for them in the pages we read.
Next door

Pumping has hurt springs nearby before.

  • A well near Kelton dropped about 25 feet from 1954 to 1967.
  • In 1967, the state stopped approving new water filings in Curlew Valley.
  • A 2009 state study tied the drop at Locomotive Springs mostly to 40 years of more farm pumping, plus a dry decade.
Heat and storms

A lot more heat, next to a lake that already stirs up storms.

A Utah State University physicist estimates the full project could make nights in the valley warmer by

8–12°F, up to 28°F

Weather scientists say the Great Salt Lake's breeze helps set up thunderstorms. The 1999 Salt Lake City tornado formed along that kind of line. Box Elder County has its own tornado record, including one in Brigham City in 1991.

Gov. Cox and the project's backers have called the heat estimates inaccurate. No one has put out a heat study of their own.

Nobody has studied what this much heat near the lake would do to storms.

What a regulator would check

Permits and reviews we couldn't find

  • Federal review: 636 acres of the water's land is federal (BLM). We found no federal environmental review started.
  • Air: the air permit is planned for 2027. A year of air testing must come first.
  • Wastewater: no discharge permit filed, and no outfall chosen.
  • Wetlands: a 2.5-mile pipe toward the lake, near marshes. No wetlands permit found.
  • Drinking water: the state hasn't confirmed the spring can reliably supply 4,850 workers.
  • Wildlife, fuel storage, the gas line and earthquake design: no reviews found yet.
The grade

Overall: D−

  • Power: D. A check on power was weakened.
  • Transparency: D. The filing is public, but the key studies aren't.
  • Who pays: D. Costs to neighbors aren't addressed.
  • Environment: F. No study of the faults, the spring over a year, heat, noise or weather.
  • Community: process C, effect D. Anyone can protest, but nearby ranches and workers would carry effects no one has studied.

A C means the law's minimum was met. D means a public check is weakened. F means something is missing, or a check was removed with nothing in its place.

To be fair

What the developer says, and what's working as it should

  • The developer says the project would use no more than 1,009 acre-feet and send more water toward the Great Salt Lake than reaches it today. That's their claim. The state hasn't ruled on it.
  • The filing went through the normal public notice, and anyone can protest.
  • The developer says it will measure its water use and report it publicly.
What no one has studied

We couldn't find any of these.

  • A full study of the valley's underground water since 1971.
  • A study of what an earthquake could do to Salt Wells Spring.
  • A heat, noise or weather study for this project.
  • The developer's "geotechnical studies." They aren't public.
  • Contact with the state's geologists. The Utah Geological Survey said neither MIDA nor the county reached out before approving the project.
Questions to ask

Who to ask, and what

  • State Engineer (by Dec. 1): Will you look at the faults and the spring over a whole year, not just two summer visits?
  • Utah Geological Survey: What are your new wells at Salt Wells Road for? Will you publish the water levels?
  • MIDA: Will you make the geotechnical studies public and require a fault study?
  • Utah Division of Air Quality: Which kind of engine are you permitting?
  • Utah OSHA and MIDA: Their own crew stopped work over heat. What protects the 4,850 workers if it gets hotter?
  • Box Elder County: Has anyone asked for a heat, noise or weather study?
