Hansel Valley's Springs, Faults and Weather: The Questions No One Has Studied

A data center campus and a 7,500-megawatt power plant want the water from Salt Wells Spring. Here is what the public record says about the ground, the water and the weather there, and what nobody has studied yet.

The short version: The springs in Hansel Valley sit on active faults. The 1934 earthquake turned springs on and off there. Next door, groundwater pumping has been tied to a spring's decline. The water filing for this project argues that other water users won't be harmed. We found no study, in that filing or anywhere public, of what the project could mean for the faults, the springs over a full year, the valley's heat, its noise, or its weather.

What this file is not: It doesn't say the project will cause earthquakes, dry up the springs or make tornadoes. It lays out what the record shows and where the record is empty.

What a protest can address now. Since H.B. 60, the State Engineer "may only consider" a plan's effect on "the beneficial use of water" or "the quantity, quality, or availability of water," plus factors other laws direct when weighing public welfare. The State Engineer also may not reject an application on public-welfare grounds for a harm that is "better suited to be addressed by another regulatory agency."[40] The other tests in Utah Code 73-3-8 still apply, including whether the change would impair existing water rights. Protests that speak to water supply, water quality and harm to other water users fit what the State Engineer weighs. Heat, air and noise are handled, if at all, by other agencies (see section 08, "What a regulator would check").

Who else uses this water

The developer's hydrology report lists the other water rights at Salt Wells Flat. Besides Bar H's own rights, they include Bureau of Land Management rights for stock water and for wildlife and wetland enhancement, and small stock-water rights on Salt Wells Stream held by a private ranching company. The report says: "No wells have active or approved water rights with the exception of monitoring wells and cathodic protection wells." One of the eight parcels in the water right's place of use, 636 acres, is federal land managed by the Bureau of Land Management.[3]

03The springs sit on active faults

The water in Salt Wells Spring is groundwater coming to the surface. The developer's own hydrologist says so: "Groundwater discharges through springs," and in the mountains water moves "through permeable fracture zones... discharging to mountain front springs."[3] The older public record shows how those springs behave when the faults move.

"There were also sightings of new springs and streams changing course. Artesian wells that had been long dry began flowing with water. Other wells, active prior to the earthquake, ceased to flow for several hours."University of Utah Seismograph Stations, on the March 12, 1934 M6.6 earthquake [4]
"Many springs formed in the epicentral area. Most of them developed along well-defined fractures in the salt flats where water flowed along the fissure." … "Precise leveling surveys later revealed that areas of land sank as much as 39 cm."U.S. Geological Survey, Professional Paper 1527 (Stover & Coffman, 1993) [5]
"Nearly all thermal springs in Utah are in or near fault zones."Major Thermal Springs of Utah, 1970 [6]

How many faults does Utah have?

The Utah Geological Survey's statewide database (Map 193DM, 2003) "contains database entries for 212 faults, fault sections, and folds, and one historical surface-faulting earthquake in Utah." That one historical surface-faulting earthquake is Hansel Valley, 1934.[7] The three Hansel faults are rated Class A in the federal fault database.[8]

04What the developer's own filing says

The filing is 196 pages, with three exhibits: a hydrology report (Loughlin Water Associates, Sept. 16, 2026), a water-use report (Beairsto & Associates, Sept. 2026) and a project summary from O'Leary Digital. We read the application, its explanatory statement, and the geology, hydrogeology, field-work and summary sections of the exhibits.[3]

THE POWER PLANTTwo descriptions of the same plant
Doesn't match

The explanatory statement assumes "fifteen (15) 500 MW combined cycle natural gas power plants." The developer's engineer describes "fifteen 500 MW reciprocating-engine blocks." Utah Clean Energy's estimates for a 9 GW gas buildout run about 1,857 tons of nitrogen oxides a year for combined-cycle turbines and about 12,000 tons for reciprocating engines.[3][13]

The explanatory statement gives its own reason: the developer "has not finalized its exact power generation plans," so "for purposes of the Application, it is assuming all fifteen (15) power plants will be combined-cycle natural gas power plants."[3]

COOLING AND BLOWDOWNCooling towers, and a waste stream with no settled destination
Outfall not chosen

The engineer: "Cooling Tower Evaporation Remains a Consumptive Component." The plan uses 61,024 gallons a day, including 10,171 gallons a day of blowdown. "If blowdown is routed to a non-returning disposal pathway or return-flow credit is not accepted, depletion would increase accordingly." The explanatory statement says the developer "may ultimately be able to treat blow-down water for discharge." The engineer's summary goes further: "Treated sanitary effluent and separately treated cooling-tower blowdown are presently planned for permitted surface-water discharge; the receiving water and final outfall remain to be confirmed." The engines run on about 2.87 million gallons a day of process water, about 90% recycled on site. The report is marked "ISSUED FOR PLANNING — NOT FOR CONSTRUCTION."[3]

WHAT THE WATER PLAN COVERSEmployees, landscaping, cooling and engines
Not in the plan we read

The engineer's stated scope: "Potable water balance; irrigation and data-center cooling demand; engine process-water recycle and treatment losses; sanitary wastewater." Drinking-water demand is figured for "the full campus population of 4,850 employees." The project's development standards also allow hotels (the draft interlocal caps them at 150 rooms), restaurants, RV parks and homes in mixed-use zones. We found no line for those in the scope or summary pages we read; we did not read every page of the report.[3][27]

05What happened next door

Curlew Valley, just north and west of Hansel Valley, has a longer water record.

Kelton irrigation well1954–1967

The water level in the first irrigation well near Kelton "had declined about 25 feet (7.6 m) since the well was put into operation in 1954," by spring 1967. In the older rocks, "the abundant faults, fractures, and associated solution openings form the principal conduits through which water moves."[10]

State Engineer moratoriumSept. 7, 1967

"The State Engineer has placed a moratorium on approvals of any new application in the Curlew Valley," with no action on pending applications "until additional studies can be made and the effect of the present development be determined on the ground-water basin."[11]

Locomotive Springs2009 study

A Utah Geological Survey study found that "a substantial increase in ground-water pumping for agriculture over the past 40 years, combined with lower-than-average precipitation during the past 10 years, are the most likely causes of the declining discharge at Locomotive Springs."[12]

06Heat and weather

Heat

USU physics professor Robert Davies has published back-of-the-envelope estimates of about 16 gigawatts of heat released in Hansel Valley at full buildout. Reported results range from 2–5°F warmer days and 8–12°F warmer nights (ABC4, May 7, 2026) to "as much as 28 degrees Fahrenheit" warmer nights (Grist, May 18, 2026). The Christian Science Monitor quotes him calling it one of the largest single-site heat sources on Earth. Gov. Cox and the project's backers have called his estimates inaccurate.[38] No heat study from the developer or a state agency has been made public.[14][15][16]

Already too hot to finish the field work. The developer's own hydrologist could not revisit every monitoring site in July 2026 "due to health and safety concerns due to high forecasted temperatures."[3] That was the valley as it is today, before any power plant or data center. The project plans 4,850 employees and power generation 24 hours a day. No study we found asks what added heat would mean for the people who would work there, or for the ranches and livestock around them.

The lake already shapes storms

A National Severe Storms Laboratory paper calls the Great Salt Lake breeze "a focusing mechanism for thunderstorms."[17] The National Weather Service says that before the Aug. 11, 1999 Salt Lake City tornado, "either an old frontal boundary existed or a convergence zone had developed" from "breezes from the Great Salt Lake meeting up with the southerly winds."[18] Radar researchers found the tornado formed as "an updraft formed over weak rotation along a pre-existing boundary layer convergence line."[19] That F2 tornado killed one person, injured more than 80 and caused about $172 million in damage.[20]

What hasn't been studied: The lake already sets up the kind of boundary the 1999 tornado formed on, and Box Elder has its own tornado and waterspout record. We found no study of what adding a heat source of about 16 gigawatts near the lake's north shore would do to storms there. Tornadoes also need the right wind shear, so whether this setup would add to them is an open question, not a finding.

07Air and noise

A study EmPower Analytics Group did for the Natural Resources Defense Council, reported Oct. 28, 2026, estimates up to 45 premature deaths a year and up to $690 million a year in health costs at a full, mostly gas buildout of about 9 GW. Gov. Cox has said Phase 1 won't exceed 1.5 GW.[25] In May 2026, the state's environmental quality director said a year of background air monitoring must come before an air permit application, and that the Wasatch Front "is already out of compliance with the Clean Air Act."[26]

On noise, the draft, unsigned interlocal agreement for the project area sets limits of 55 dB(A) by day and 45 dB(A) at night "at any residential home outside the boundary of the Project Area." No noise study has been made public.[27]

08What a regulator would check

The laws that changed the rules

From 2025 to 2026, the Legislature passed laws that change who reviews a project like this and what the public can challenge. Some narrow a check, some add one. The Legislature's own August 2026 handout is titled "No single agency approves a data center." It says MIDA "exercises land-use authority under its project-area plans and development standards rather than the ordinary municipal or county land-use process," and that "separately applicable state and federal requirements continue to apply."[49] Here is what each enacted bill says, from the bill text.

09Who checked the ground

What the record shows

  • MIDA's spokesperson: "the development team did have preliminary geotechnical studies conducted before creating the project area boundary," with "many more studies" to come. Those studies aren't public.[28]
  • The Utah Geological Survey said neither MIDA nor Box Elder County contacted it before approving the project. "The Utah Geological Survey is not a regulatory agency."[28]
  • MIDA answered a records request for environmental studies by saying it had no responsive records.[29]
  • The Utah Geological Survey had three wells drilled at Salt Wells Road in November 2025, two with flowing artesian water.[3]
  • H.B. 60 (2026) narrowed what the State Engineer weighs as "public welfare" to effects on the beneficial use of water and on the quantity, quality or availability of water.[40]
  • H.B. 76 (2026) added a check that applies to large data centers: report expected water use to the Division of Water Rights before construction, and actual use every year after. The state publishes the withdrawal numbers.[42]

What we couldn't find

  • Any public groundwater or spring-flow study of Hansel Valley since 1971.
  • Any study of how fault movement could change Salt Wells Spring.
  • Lidar fault mapping of Hansel Valley. The Survey's 2026 summary lists it as still needed.[31]
  • Any public heat, noise or weather study for the project.
  • What the November 2025 Survey wells are monitoring, and whether they record water levels over time.
  • Whether H.B. 76's notice step reaches this project. It tells city and county land-use authorities to notify Water Rights and Water Quality before approving a large data center. Here, MIDA is the land-use authority, and the bill's definition doesn't name MIDA. The developer's own reporting duty applies either way.[42][49]

10The grade

We grade every case file on the Hive's five standing questions, the same way every time. The grades are a fixed published standard, not the writer's opinion. This grade covers the a55167 filing and how the project is being reviewed.

QuestionGradeWhy, from the record
PowerDH.B. 60 narrowed what the State Engineer can weigh as public welfare. MIDA and the county approved the project area without contacting the Utah Geological Survey. The protest right still exists, so the check is weakened, not removed.
TransparencyDThe full 196-page filing is public, a plus. But the "preliminary geotechnical studies" aren't public, the filing describes two different engine types, and the discharge outfall is "to be confirmed."
Financial accountabilityDCosts that could land on neighbors, such as the health costs in the NRDC-commissioned estimate and heat on nearby ranches, aren't addressed in the filing, and after H.B. 60 they fall largely outside the water decision. Who would pay is not stated.
Environmental impactFNo public study of the faults, the spring over a full year, the aquifer, heat, noise or weather. The water review's public-welfare test was narrowed, and no agency has been assigned those questions in its place.
Community impactProcess C · Effect DProcess: public notice ran and anyone can protest. Effect: ranchers, Snowville and the planned workforce would carry heat, air and water effects that no study has examined.
OverallD−Average of the five, counting community impact by its effect.

A C means the record meets what Utah law requires, the status quo. A B or A means the record goes beyond it. D means something is vague or bundled, or a public check is weakened; F means something required is missing, or a check is removed with nothing in its place.

How this could have been done better

  • Publish the developer's geotechnical studies before the water decision.
  • Measure the spring and the aquifer through a full year, not two summer visits.
  • Ask the Utah Geological Survey for a fault and seismic review before approval.
  • Name one engine type and the discharge outfall in the filing.
  • Model the heat and its effect on nearby ranches and storms.
  • Count water for every allowed use, including hotels, restaurants, RV parks and homes.

How a grade can change: If the studies are published, or the State Engineer requires them before deciding, we'll update the grade with the date.

Bottom line

The public record ties Hansel Valley's springs to active faults, shows those springs changing when the ground moved in 1934, and shows pumping linked to a spring's decline next door. The water filing argues no other water user would be harmed. The record we found holds no study of the faults, the springs over a full year, the heat, the noise or the weather for this project.
