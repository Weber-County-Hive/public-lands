The Viral Bears Ears Post, Checked Against the Record

A widely shared Facebook post about Bears Ears and Grand Staircase-Escalante makes 38 checkable claims. We checked each one against court opinions, proclamations, management plans and agency data.

The short version: Most of the post's quotes and numbers hold up, including several that sound like spin but are on the record. The errors are about timing, one board seat, coal, and the access numbers. Our read of the record: a monument's boundary decides what is protected, and its management plan decides which roads and trails are open. The post gets that distinction right, then overstates some of the numbers it uses to make the point.

About the post: It is an opinion piece shared across Facebook groups. We don't name its author. We graded only claims a record can check; lines that are the writer's opinion are marked that way.

The timing

The post is written as if the order came this week.

CLAIM 01The order came “on Monday” and this is the week everyone is posting about it.
Not supported

The proclamations were signed July 13, 2026. This week's news is different: a federal judge in Washington, D.C. reopened the 2017 lawsuits over the first cuts, and ordered 48 hours' notice before ground-disturbing work on the removed lands. A reshared copy of the post reads as current when it isn't.

“The largest rollback in history”

The post's history of the two monuments.

CLAIM 02Clinton designated Grand Staircase in 1996, signing it from the rim of the Grand Canyon in Arizona.
Supported

The National Park Service's report for Sept. 23, 1996 says Clinton spoke near El Tovar Lodge on the canyon's rim and signed the proclamation at the Powell Memorial.

Sources: [2]
CLAIM 03Five maps in thirty years: 1996, 2016, 2017, 2021, 2026, and about 3.25 million acres moving back and forth without a vote in Congress.
Supported

The dates match the record. Biden's 2021 proclamations reserved 3.23 million acres for the two monuments together, according to the Tenth Circuit opinion. All five changes were made by presidential proclamation under the Antiquities Act.

Sources: [3] [1]

“The courts have settled this”

What the Tenth Circuit actually decided.

CLAIM 04The Tenth Circuit sent the challenge back, said courts can review whether a president exceeded the Antiquities Act, and told the district court to interpret the law's limits “in the first instance.”
Supported

Accurate. On June 23, 2026 the court affirmed in part, vacated in part and remanded. The opinion says the Act “places discernible limits” on the President and that courts would “undermine rather than honor congressional intent” by refusing to enforce them. Two things the post leaves out: it was a 2–1 decision with a written dissent (Judge Federico), and the court did not decide whether the monuments actually exceeded the limits.

Sources: [3]
CLAIM 05“Nobody had done it. Not once, in a hundred and twenty years.”
Partly

Partly. The majority notes the U.S. Supreme Court has reviewed presidential actions under the Antiquities Act three times (1920, 1976, 1978). What no court of appeals had done, according to the district court's own order quoted in the opinion, is interpret the “smallest area compatible” requirement.

Sources: [3]
CLAIM 06BlueRibbon Coalition was part of that case, alongside the State of Utah.
Partly

BlueRibbon was a plaintiff. But the Tenth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of BlueRibbon's and the individual plaintiffs' claims because they did not argue standing in their opening brief. Utah, Garfield County and Kane County remain in the case.

Sources: [3]
CLAIM 07Chief Justice Roberts wrote in 2021 that the “smallest area” restriction had “ceased to pose any meaningful restraint.”
Supported

Accurate quote. It came from a statement respecting the denial of certiorari in Massachusetts Lobstermen's Ass'n v. Raimondo (2021). It is not a ruling.

Sources: [3] [4]
CLAIM 08Bryce Canyon 35,835 acres; Arches 76,679; Zion 146,597; Capitol Reef 241,904; Canyonlands 337,598. Bears Ears 1.36 million; Grand Staircase 1.87 million.
Supported

The National Park Service's official acreage listing matches for Bryce Canyon, Arches, Capitol Reef and Canyonlands. The Zion figure matches a published listing; we did not read it in the NPS listing itself. The monument figures are their sizes before July 13, 2026.

Sources: [5] [1]

“A gift to oil and mining”

Energy and mineral claims.

CLAIM 09The Western Energy Alliance said Bears Ears has never been a significant oil and natural gas prospect, in the New York Times article everyone is sharing.
Supported

Accurate. The statement came from Melissa Simpson, the group's president, in the New York Times article of July 14, 2026.

Sources: [6]
CLAIM 10A 2021 Utah Geological Survey report found low to moderate energy mineral potential in both monuments.
Supported

Accurate. These are two March 2021 Utah Geological Survey resource overviews (Public Information Series 102 for Bears Ears and 103 for Grand Staircase). The Grand Staircase report also rates coal potential as high.

Sources: [7] [8]
CLAIM 11“Grand Staircase does sit on coal.” The removed land isn't about mining.
Partly

The coal part is accurate. But both July 2026 proclamations themselves cite critical minerals and national security as grounds, and the removed lands opened to mining claims and mineral leasing 60 days later (Sept. 11). Utah's mining association president said there is “definitely potential.”

Sources: [1] [9] [10]
CLAIM 12“Coal use in this country falls every single year.”
Not supported

Not supported. The U.S. Energy Information Administration forecast a 6% increase in U.S. coal consumption for 2025, followed by a decrease in 2026. The long-term trend is down, but not every year.

The Wyss section

Claims about Hansjörg Wyss, the Swiss donor.

CLAIM 13Hansjörg Wyss is Swiss, born in Bern, made his fortune in medical devices, and sold the company to Johnson & Johnson for over $20 billion.
Supported

Accurate. Johnson & Johnson's 2012 annual report says it completed the acquisition of Synthes for a purchase price of $20.2 billion in cash and stock.

Sources: [12] [13]
CLAIM 14He has given more than $350 million to groups shaping public lands policy, and his foundation holds around $2.5 billion.
Partly

The $350 million figure is from a 2015 E&E News profile; a later figure puts public-land giving above $450 million as of 2018. The $2.5 billion figure is not supported by what we found: the foundation's endowment was reported at $1.71 billion as of 2023.

Sources: [14] [15]
CLAIM 15He sits on the board of directors of the Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance. “It's on Wyss' own Wikipedia, that lists him as current Board of Directors.”
Partly

Past tense is accurate: SUWA's 2005 newsletter says he had served on its board for ten years and as chair for seven, and he was still listed in its Autumn/Winter 2020 newsletter. Present tense is not: SUWA's own board page (updated April 2026) lists ten directors and two emeriti, and he is not among them. The Wikipedia listing appears to be out of date.

Sources: [16] [17] [18]

Supported

Accurate. SUWA's executive director said in the July 14 New York Times article that the group would challenge the decision in federal court.

Partly

The Wilderness Society's governing council page lists him (at large, since 1993). For the Grand Canyon Trust, a business-records listing names him as a trustee, but we could not load the Trust's own board page to confirm it.

“A monument doesn't take away access”

The management plan, the V Road and the Cosmic Ashtray.

CLAIM 22The proclamation doesn't manage the land; the management plan does, and it comes later.
Supported

Accurate. Each proclamation directs the agency to write a management plan, and the plan sets roads, grazing, camping and other uses. Grand Staircase's first plan came in 2000; the current one was signed Jan. 6, 2025. Bears Ears' plan was signed the same month.

Sources: [24] [25]
CLAIM 23The 2025 Grand Staircase plan designated about 1.23 million acres “primitive,” two-thirds of the monument, closed to motorized access.
Partly

The acreage is accurate (about 1,227,100 acres). “Closed to motorized access” goes too far: the plan's record of decision says that apart from the V Road, it keeps the existing travel network from the 2020 plans. The primitive areas are closed to off-route driving, not to every road.

Sources: [25] [26]
CLAIM 24Public comments and local governments' input were ignored.
Unconfirmed / opinion

Not something a record can settle. The plan followed a two-year public process with more than 30 engagement meetings; whether input was “ignored” is an opinion.

Sources: [27]
CLAIM 25“The V Road is seven miles.”
Not supported

Not supported. Driving guides put the V Road itself at about 3.25 miles. The Harris Wash road and the V Road together run about 9.7 miles from Hole-in-the-Rock Road.

Sources: [28] [29]
CLAIM 26You used to drive the V Road, park, and walk about two miles round trip to the Cosmic Ashtray; the 2025 plan shut the road.
Supported

Accurate. From the end of the V Road the approach is about 1.1 miles each way, and the 2025 plan closed the V Road.

Sources: [29] [26]
CLAIM 27“That two-mile hike is now roughly twenty miles round trip.”
Not supported

Not supported. Without the V Road, the southern approach from Harris Wash is about 9 miles round trip, and the approach from Spencer Flat Road is about 8.3 miles round trip.

Sources: [30]
CLAIM 28The July order came with no new management plan, so the Biden plans still stand.
Partly

Partly. Congress's attempt to throw out the 2025 Grand Staircase plan missed its fast-track deadline in June 2026, so that plan was not repealed. But the 2026 Bears Ears proclamation sets its own interim road rules: until a new plan is done, agencies may allow motorized use on routes designated before 2016 and may add routes. Interior has one year to update the plans. We could not confirm which plan governs the removed Grand Staircase lands today.

Sources: [31] [32] [9] [10]
CLAIM 29“The shrink didn't open a single gate.”
Not supported

Not supported for mining and leasing: the removed lands opened to mining claims and mineral and geothermal leasing on Sept. 11, 2026, and prospectors filed claims. For roads, see the item above.

Sources: [9] [33]

“An attack on Native Americans”

The Chuckwalla proclamation.

CLAIM 33The Chuckwalla proclamation (California, January 2025) describes trails Tribes “evoke and visit through songs and dreams,” and finds its 624,270 acres are “the smallest area compatible.”
Supported

Accurate quotes from Proclamation 10881, signed Jan. 14, 2025.

Sources: [35]
CLAIM 34The same proclamation carves out room for renewable energy and preserves utility corridors, transmission lines and new facilities.
Supported

Accurate, with context. Existing utility and transmission facilities may be kept, expanded, and new ones built “to the extent consistent with the proper care and management of the objects.” The renewable energy language covers projects in development areas the proclamation describes as near the monument, not inside it.

Sources: [35]
CLAIM 35“The land is too sacred for a Jeep.”
Not supported

Not supported by the proclamation. It lists motorized recreation among the area's uses and allows motorized vehicles on roads and trails already in BLM's route inventory.

Sources: [35]
CLAIM 36Tribal opinion is not one thing; there are Native voices on every side.
Unconfirmed / opinion

An opinion, not graded. The five tribes of the Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition condemned the July reduction.

03Who decides what you can drive to

The post's main point is that the boundary and the road rules are two different documents. That part is right. Here is how the pieces fit, as of October 2026.

The chain of documents
flowchart TD
  A["Proclamation
sets the boundary and
what is protected"] --> B["Management plan
sets grazing, camping,
which areas allow vehicles"]
  B --> C["Travel network
which roads and trails
are open"]
  A --> D["Land removed from
a monument"]
  D --> E["Back to the BLM office's
regular plan; open to mining
claims and leasing after 60 days"]

Text version: The proclamation sets the boundary. The management plan, written later, sets uses inside it, including which roads stay open. Land removed from a monument goes back to the BLM's regular management and, under the July 2026 proclamations, opened to mining claims and leasing after 60 days.

What the record shows

  • The 2025 Grand Staircase plan closed one designated route, the V Road, and kept the rest of the existing road network.
  • Congress's fast-track attempt to throw out that plan missed its June 2026 deadline.
  • The July 2026 Bears Ears proclamation lets agencies allow driving on pre-2016 routes until a new plan is done.
  • Interior has one year from July 13, 2026 to update the plans.

What we couldn't confirm

  • Which plan governs the removed Grand Staircase lands today, and whether the V Road closure still applies there.
  • Whether the Volcano/V Road area falls inside or outside the new Grand Staircase boundary.

Bottom line

The post is right that boundaries change with each president and that the management plan, not the line on the map, decides most access. It is wrong or out of date on the timing, Wyss's current SUWA seat, coal use, the size of the road closures, and the hike distance.
